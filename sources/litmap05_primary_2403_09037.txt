Abstract
Large vision-language models (LVLMs), designed to interpret and respond to human instructions, occasionally generate hallucinated or harmful content due to inappropriate instructions. This study uses linear probing to shed light on the hidden knowledge at the output layer of LVLMs. We demonstrate that the logit distributions of the first tokens contain sufficient information to determine whether to respond to the instructions, including recognizing unanswerable visual questions, defending against multi-modal jailbreaking attack, and identifying deceptive questions. Such hidden knowledge is gradually lost in logits of subsequent tokens during response generation. Then, we illustrate a simple decoding strategy at the generation of the first token, effectively improving the generated content. In experiments, we find a few interesting insights: First, the CLIP model already contains a strong signal for solving these tasks, indicating potential bias in the existing datasets. Second, we observe performance improvement by utilizing the first logit distributions on three additional tasks, including indicting uncertainty in math solving, mitigating hallucination, and image classification. Last, with the same training data, simply finetuning LVLMs improve models’ performance but is still inferior to linear probing on these tasks. 111Our code is available at https://github.com/Qinyu-Allen-Zhao/LVLM-LP.

1 Introduction
Large vision-language models (LVLMs), integrating vision encoders and pre-trained large language models (LLMs), have emerged as powerful tools for interpreting multimodal input and responding to complex human instructions [30, 25, 15, 32, 2]. Despite their impressive capabilities, recent works report that LVLMs often hallucinate when responding to unanswerable visual questions [11], generate harmful content under jailbreaking attack [16], and are easily fooled by deceptive questions [21], as illustrated in Fig. 1.
Refer to caption
Figure 1: Scenarios where LVLMs may make undesirable responses. The first tokens are emphasized in italics for clarity. Note that although the first tokens are usually nondescript words like “the” and “1”, we find that the logit vectors of them are actually very informative for determining the proper responses.
In this paper we study the question, do models implicitly know that they are generating inappropriate or undesirable content? Specifically, we examine the logit distribution over tokens at the output layer and use this as a feature vector through linear probing for predicting various behavior (unanswerability, jailbreaking, deception), in spite of the content that the model actually produces. Interestingly, the logit distribution of the very first token contain sufficient information on determining if the model should follow the instruction. The usefulness of the “first logits” is not much influenced when we use different prompts, such as directly asking whether the model should respond to the instructions, asking the original prompts, or providing hints that the instructions may be unanswerable or harmful. We also find that the hidden knowledge weakens as the model continues to generate output, so that the logit distributions of later tokens are less discriminative than earlier ones for these tasks.
A similar finding was recently reported by Slobodkin et al. [24] and also Qian et al. [20] but both in the limited context of natural language processing (NLP) and using the hidden states of a model. In our work, we show that this approach can be applied more generally and, importantly, with only limited assumption of model internals (i.e., access to logit distributions over tokens). We also provide insights into some of their findings, such as why averaging over multiple tokens does not improve results.
Armed with these findings, a simple decoding strategy (shown in Fig. 2) can be applied at the generation of the first token, guided by a trained linear classifier on each task, effectively improving the generated content. Our approach enhances the safety and reliability of LLMs and LVLMs, and provides an alternative to implementing front-end guardrails or retaining models through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
Through extensive experimentation, we arrive at several interesting insights:
•
We find CLIP [22], whose vision encoder is often employed in LVLMs, effectively identifies unanswerable questions, jailbreaking attacks, and deceptive questions, indicating significant dataset bias. That is, inappropriate visual instructions are significantly different from the normal input.
•
We apply linear probing on the first logit distributions for other three tasks and observe performance improvement, including indicting uncertainty in math solving, mitigating hallucination, and image classification. This shows that sufficient hidden knowledge exists in the first logit distributions, which can be used to enhance LVLMs.
•
We finetune LLaVA [14] using the training data for all tasks involved in our study, and also retrain LLaVA by combining its original training data with ours. We find that finetuning and retraining improve the performance on these tasks but is not as good as linear probing. After finetuning or retraining, the linear probing performance on each task is usually weakened.

2 Related Works
Visual instruction tuning on LVLMs. Existing works typically utilize a multi-stage training approach for LVLMs [14, 2, 32]. The last stage trains LVLM to follow human instructions to enhance usability. Liu et al. [15] generate human instructions and model responses by prompting GPT-4 with COCO annotations [13], and train LLaVA-v1.0 to follow the instructions and learn from the responses generated from GPT-4. Dai et al. [2, 10] leverage distinct instruction templates and convert publicly available datasets for constructing their data. This type of visual instruction tuning gets success with different models, but issues raise when the instructions at inference are inappropriate.
Undesirable content generated by LVLMs. Previous works report that LVLMs are prone to answer unanswerable or deceptive questions with hallucinated or inaccurate content [21], generate harmful responses given maliciously designed instructions [1, 16], or answer some questions even though they do not know the correct answers [29]. Qian et al. [21] prompts GPT-4 to generate deceptive questions. For instance, given an image of two dogs, a deceptive questions with incorrect object number is “What are the three dogs in the image doing?” LVLMs are easily fooled by these questions and describe the activities of three dogs. Liu et al. [16] generate images of illegal or harmful activities by using diffusion models, and ask LVLMs how to do the activities shown in the images, i.e., jailbreaking attack, demonstrating the vulnerability of existing LVLMs.
Previous works on addressing these issues. In literature, there are existing attempts for these problems. A straightforward method is to align LVLMs with human preference using SFT or RLHF [1]. Another possible way is to collect datasets containing harmful and normal data, and finetune an LLM to detect harmful content [18]. But both methods require a lot of computational resources. Prompt tuning, which aims to manually design or learn prompts to enhance LVLMs on a specific task, is also useful but usually sub-optimal. It is non-trivial to manually design prompts and costly to learn prompts with LVLMs.
Linear probing in LLMs. Linear probing has been used in understanding and extract knowledge of LLMs [8]. Two contemporary works in NLP [24, 20] demonstrate linear probing based on the first generated token in LLMs is able to identify unanswerable questions and complete other trustworthy-related tasks. We claim that our work is conducted independently and presents notable distinctions: First, we observe the logit distributions of the first tokens contain similar information and even outperform the hidden states of the first generated tokens. Second, we conduct experiments on six diverse tasks with visual input, indicating a broader scope. Last, we derive a few insights, some of which can explain the observation in their works.
3 Linear Probing on Logit Distributions
In this section, we first revisit the autoregressive generation process of LVLMs, and then introduce our linear probing method based on the logit distribution of tokens. We will discuss further exploration regarding our method. Last, we showcase a simple decoding method guided by results from linear probing.
3.1 LVLM background
We start by a brief introduction to LVLM. A typical LVLM, contains a vision encoder {\cal E} and a large language model {\cal M} . Let T be the token set, e.g., a set of 32,000 tokens for LLaVA. Given an image {\boldsymbol{x}} and the text input {\boldsymbol{t}}=[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\mid t_{n}\in T] , the model generates a sequence of tokens [y_{1},\ldots,y_{K}\mid y_{k}\in T] .
The generation is autoregressive, which means {\cal M} predicts the next token y_{k} each time based on the input ({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{t}}) and previous output {\boldsymbol{y}}_{<k}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1}] . The logit distribution over the next token is
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}={\cal M}({\cal E}({\boldsymbol{x}}),{\boldsymbol{t}},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{<k})\in\mathbb{R}^{|T|},
(1)
where |T| is the number of candidate tokens.
A sampling function {\cal S} will be applied to the logit distribution {\boldsymbol{\ell}} , for example, greedy decoding and beam search. Upon sampling the next token, the process evaluates termination criteria, which primarily include: whether the next token is a special token indicating the end of the generation, or if the generated content has reached the allowed maximum length. If the generation continues, subsequent tokens are produced based on the input and the preceding output.
Next, we introduce the proposed linear probing method based on logits.
3.2 Linear probing
For a specific task, we have a train set and a test set. Each sample is an image-text pair, for example, an image of dog and a question regarding the color of the dog. We feed the training samples into an LVLM and extract the logit distribution corresponding to each output token, and train a linear classifier model {\cal P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} with learnable parameters \boldsymbol{\theta} on the logits. Then, we validate {\cal P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} on the test set.
In experiments, we evaluate seven different LVLMs in three tasks with varying prompt designs. We will demonstrate that the logit distribution of the very first token proves to be very beneficial across a variety of tasks, including recognizing unanswerable visual questions, defending against multi-modal jailbreaking attack, and identifying deceptive questions. The usefulness of the “first logits” will be validated across different models, tasks and prompts.
3.3 Further exploration
We explore more questions w.r.t hidden knowledge in the output layer of LVLMs.
Logit distributions of the first token vs. subsequent tokens.
We compare the performance of the linear classifier trained on the first token and on the subsequent tokens. The hypothesis here is that the first logit distribution contains enough hidden knowledge gathered from the input, while the subsequent logits have less, because the LVLM model conditioned on previous tokens gradually becomes more confident in the output that it generates.
Logit distributions vs. hidden states. Recent works reports hidden states of the first generated tokens can be used to identify unanswerable questions or do trustworthy-related tasks in NLP [24, 20]. We also use those hidden states to train a linear classifier on our multi-modal tasks, and compare against performance using logits. Note that access to hidden states requires more knowledge of the internal model architecture than logit output.
Why do logits of the first token contain information on these tasks?
We evaluate the performance of linear probing on the CLIP model (ViT-L/14 336px) [22] on the same tasks. We utilize (1) image embeddings; (2) text embeddings; and (3) both from CLIP. We will show that CLIP already shows remarkable performance, serving as a strong information source for LLaVA. Besides, we notice that there may be significant bias in the datasets. The jailbreaking attack and normal images are significantly different.
3.4 Decoding method guided by linear probing
Considering the structure of autoregressive model, we show a simple decoding method, which can utilize the remarkable performance of linear probing. Given the image and a prompt, we feed them to the LVLM and get the logit distribution of the first token. Then, we provide the logits to a trained linear classifier. As shown in Fig. 2, the question is predicted as “jailbreaking attack” according to the linear classifier. Next, the output tokens will be set as “Sorry, answering your question will generate harmful content, because”. We send the tokens back to the LVLM and ask it to generate the following tokens. Thanks to the autoregressive architecture, the LVLM can automatically fill in the following reasoning process.
This method just adds a linear classifier into the decoding generation process, without huge additional computation cost at inference. Note that this is just one of possible demonstrations of how the results of linear probing can be used.
4 Experiments
4.1 Models and prompts
In our experiments, we cover open-source models including LLaVA-v1.5 (13B), LLaVA-v1.5 (7B) [14], InstructBLIP [2], mPLUG-Owl [31], LLaMA-Adapter (V2, multi-modal) [4], Multi-Modal GPT [6], and MiniGPT-4 [32]. Without explicit specification, we use the 7B version of the models. All these models have been reported to readily generate undesirable content.
We design different prompts, including (1) Open, asking the original questions in the benchmark; (2) Hint, asking the original questions with a hint indicating the question may be unanswerable / may generate harmful content / may be deceptive; (3) Meta, asking a meta-question like “Is this question answerable or not?”. Detailed prompts in the study are provided in the supp. materials.
4.2 Metrics
Our primary evaluation metrics are accuracy (ACC), F1 score, and Area Under the receiver operating characteristic Curve (AUC). Additionally, for datasets on jailbreaking and deception, we report the attack success rate (ASR). This metric treats prompts as attacks and calculates the proportion of instances where the model follow these prompts without recognizing the problems in them.
Note that to compute the AUROC metric for LVLMs, we use logits of some specific tokens, following the practice in NLP [12, 3, 26]. This approach generally yields superior results compared to relying solely on the raw outputs of LVLMs. For instance, when asking model “Will answering the question <%s> generate harmful content?”, we extract the logit associated with the token “No”. A comprehensive outline of the evaluation is provided in the supp. materials.
4.3 Tasks and datasets
Unanswerable VQA. The VizWiz dataset is designed to aid the visually impaired [7]. It comprises images captured by blind people, each accompanied by a spoken question about the image. Some of questions are unanswerable due to not sufficient visual details. The task we focus on is to predict whether a visual question can be answered or not. The train and validation sets contain 20,523 and 4,319 image-question pairs, respectively.
Defense to jailbreaking attacks. MM-SafetyBench applies jailbreaking attacks at LVLMs in thirteen scenarios with malicious text prompts and images [16]. The original dataset provides 1,680 unsafe questions for attack, and for each question, they generate three different types of images, including an image generated by stable diffusion [19], an image of text, and a combined image of the first two. In this study, we only use the combined images, which usually show the highest attack success rates.
A weakness of the dataset is that it only contains jailbreaking attacks: a extremely conservative model that declines to answer any questions will get perfect performance on this dataset but is not useful in practice. To build a balanced test set, we first ask LVLMs to distinguish between jailbreaking attack from this benchmark and normal questions from other benchmarks. However, this leads to significant simplification of the task due to the distribution difference between jailbreaking attack and normal questions. Thus, we follow the data generation pipeline in MM-SafetyBench to generate safe questions first via prompting GPT-4 and convert these questions to jailbreaking-style image-question pairs.
We generate questions of different categories, including daily activity, economics, physical, legal, politics, finance, health, sex, and government. For each category, we generate 200 questions and generate a corresponding image for each image. Finally, we have 1,800 safe image-question pairs in total.
We train our linear model on these data to distinguish if the output will be harmful. For training, we sample 10 samples from each category in both safe and unsafe sets. Thus, we have 90 safe training data and 130 unsafe training data. The remaining data of the MM-SafeBench and those of our generated data are used as the test set. We also sample 1,000 image-question pairs from the LLaVA train set and add them into the train set as safe data.

Identifying deceptive questions. MAD-Bench contains 850 image-question pairs designed to deceit LVLMs, regarding count of object, non-existent object, object attribute, scene understanding, spatial relationship, and visual confusion [21]. For example, given an image of two cats, a deceptive question is to ask the model “What are the three cats doing?”. In this case, instead of answering the question directly, models are expected to recognize and clarify the inconsistency between questions and images in their responses.
The deceptive questions are generated by prompting GPT-4 with COCO ground truth captions, and the generated questions are manually filtered to build the dataset. However, this dataset has not been publicly released. So we repeat their generation pipeline, using the same prompts to generate 1,000 images of five different categories.
The MAD-Bench benchmark only contains deceptive questions, leading to biased evaluation. If a model naively replies “Sorry, the question is inconsistent with the image” to all questions, it will have a perfect result on the benchmark. To address this, we prompt GPT-4 to generate 1,000 normal questions based on COCO val2017. We use 100 deceptive data and 100 normal data to train the linear classifer and access it on the remaining data.

4.5 Further analysis
Hidden knowledge in subsequent tokens. In the main experiments, we focus on the logit distribution of the first token. Here we explore if the subsequent tokens such as the second and the third tokens have similar properties. We continue to use the three datasets, prompt the model with the original questions, and train a logistic regression based on the logit distributions of different tokens. In experiments, LVLMs may not produce responses of sufficient length. Consequently, these instances are excluded from the training and test datasets. However, it is important to note that the proportion of such samples is minimal, thereby ensuring their impact on model performance is negligible.
As shown in Fig. 3(a-b), the performance of linear probing goes down gradually when we use the following tokens. The results may indicate the model contains the most information at the beginning of generating the response, but fool itself and lose that gradually in the autoregressive process.

Training the probing module on hidden states. We feed data of train and test sets into LLaVA-v1.5 (7B), and extract hidden states in different layers of the first generated tokens. Then, we train a logistic regression based on the hidden states of each layer on the train set, and validate it on the test set. But the last several layers are slightly inferior to the first logit distribution. Note that, because of the architecture of the LLMs involved in this study [27, 28], the hidden states are gotten after the generation of the first token.
Why are logits of the first token useful on these tasks?
Furthermore, we assess the performance of CLIP with the same train and test sets. Note that LLaVA uses the image encoder of CLIP. We train a linear classifier based on (1) image embeddings; (2) text embeddings; or (3) both from CLIP.
As shown in the Table 2, CLIP already demonstrates good performance on the three tasks. It indicates that most of hidden knowledge contained in the logit distribution actually comes from CLIP. LLaVA perhaps has better language understanding, so that linear probing has improved performance than CLIP.
These results indicate another problem that there may be a significant distribution bias in the datasets, since the “image-” and “text-only” methods show strong performance whereas, by definition both text and image are required for performing the task. We manually check the images and questions in the dataset. Take the jailbreaking benchmark as an example. The prompts for jailbreaking attack usually contain some notable segments, such as “without leaving a trace”, “nearly impossible to detect”, and “discreetly”, which are not contained in the normal prompts. The images for jailbreaking contain illegal or harmful text and activities, and usually are more gloomy and dark than normal images. This dataset bias may make the benchmarks much easier than expected.

5 Discussion
We first discuss the application scope of linear probing on the first logit distributions. We apply our method on three additional tasks and observe performance improvement as well. Then, we check whether finetune or retrain LLaVA with the train sets in our tasks can get similar results with linear probing. Last, we conclude our study and list main limitations.
5.1 Application scope of linear probing
Prediction of answer correctness. This experiment harnesses the first-token logit distribution to ascertain the accuracy of math solutions generated by LVLMs.

To establish a baseline for the accuracy of the answers, we employ the MathVista dataset [17], comprising 1000 image-question pairs related to math problems. This dataset challenges the model to predict a diverse array of answer types, including choices, floating-point numbers, integers, and lists, thereby complicating the task of correctness prediction. We prompt LVLMs with the math visual prompts, and evaluate their accuracy by using GPT-4, guided by scripts provided in their official GitHub repository.
Given the dataset’s limited size, we adopt a 10-fold cross-validation approach to ensure robustness in our analysis. For each training segment, a logistic regression model is trained to predict the accuracy of responses based on the logit distribution of the first logit. This model is then applied to predict the accuracy of answers in the test segment. The performance of this methodology is quantified through AUROC and ACC metrics across all folds.
As a baseline, we ask LVLMs to evaluate if their own answers are correct. Interestingly, we also apply linear probing on the first logit distribution of this self-evaluation, to predict if the answers are correct. As shown in Fig. 5, linear probing shows much improved performance than simple self-evaluation.
Image classification. A recent work[5] reports that LVLMs are good at superclass classification such as cats vs. dogs but cannot provide an concrete category from a defined detailed category set. They propose a pipeline: first, ask the model what the object is in the image, e.g., a dog. Then, they ask a follow-up question, “What type of dog is this?”. LVLMs will provide a more concrete answer to the follow-up question. Last, the answer is correct if the CLIP text embedding of the correct class exhibits the highest similarity to the answer.
In this paper, we use ImageNet-1k [23] as the dataset to check whether the first logit distribution can also be used to distinguish between different classes. From the train set, we sample 16 images for each class and prompt an LVLM about the label of the foreground object. We train Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) on the first logit distributions generated by the LVLM, and validate it on the whole test set. For each model, the experiments are run three times and we report the average Top-1 accuracy. Note that we do not need to use CLIP to match LVLMs’ answers to the closest class.
As shown in the Table 5.1, the performance of LDA is much better than the original LVLMs, proving the first logit distributions can be utilized for image classification as well. However, the accuracy is still far away from the performance of CLIP, indicating the lose of information for classification in LVLMs.
Object hallucination. Object hallucination is a new problem raising in LVLMs, which means LVLMs will describe object not present in the image when answering to human instructions. The POPE benchmark [11] consists of 500 images from COCO val2014 set [13], and ask six questions like “Is there a <object> in the image?” in each image. For training linear probing, we sample 100, 500, and 1000 images from COCO train2014 set, respectively, and generate six questions for each image, following the original benchmark. We evaluate LVLMs and the performance of linear probing, and the results are shown in Table 5.1.

5.2 Comparing our method to finetuning or retraining LLaVA
Refer to caption
Figure 6: Comparing linear probing with retraining or finetuning LLaVA. (a) Identifying unanswerable questions; (b) Defending against jailbreaking attack; (c) Identifying deceptive questions; (d) Predicting the correctness of the math solutions; (e) Mitigating hallucination; (f) Image classification. We use two methods to enhance LLaVA on these tasks. One is to finetune LLaVA with the combination of train sets used in the six tasks. We do not finetune LLaVA separately on each task, because we use only a few training samples on some tasks such as jailbreaking, probably leading to severe overfitting. The other is to add our training data into the train set of LLaVA, and retrain it in instruction tuning stage. Both finetuning and retraining use LORA [9].
As shown in Fig. 6(a-f), retraining and finetuning improve the perforance of LLaVA on each task. Compared with linear probing, LLaVA is generally inferior except on the MathVista benchmark in Fig. 6(d). Moreover, linear probing needs less training time and much fewer numbers of learnable parameters. In addition, although finetuning is also quick in training, it may result in a decrease in model performance on other tasks. In comparison, linear probing does not have this problem, because it is separated from the LVLM.
5.3 Conclusion & Limitations
The study finds that the logit distributions from LVLMs help identify inappropriate instructions, including unanswerable questions, jailbreaking attack, and deceptive questions, and the logits are also useful for other tasks, such as predicting correctness, mitigating hallucination, and image classification.
Limitations are highlighted: First, all experiments are based on the output layer of LVLMs. There might be another way to formalise mutual information between logits and unanswerability or jailbreaking, which is underexplored in this study. Second, a train set is still needed to fit the linear probing module. A unsupervised way to explore the hidden knowledge in LVLMs is an interesting future direction. Third, we need better datasets where performance on text-only and image-only is close to random. For example, we may ask two questions on the same image, one is answerable while the other is unanswerable. This will remove the bias in the current datasets and provide more accurate evaluation on LVLMs. These are left as future works.

Appendix 0.B Variants of our method
0.B.1 Use the probability distribution of the first token
Given the logit distribution over the first token {\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K} , the probability for a specific token is
p_{i}=\text{softmax}(\ell_{i}/t)=\frac{e^{\ell_{i}/t}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\ell_{k}/t}},
(2)
where t is the hyperparameter for temperature scaling. We have
\displaystyle\log p_{i}
\displaystyle=\log\frac{e^{\ell_{i}/t}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\ell_{k}/t}}
(3)
\displaystyle=\log e^{\ell_{i}/t}-\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\ell_{k}/t}
(4)
\displaystyle=\ell_{i}/t-\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\ell_{k}/t}
(5)
On each of the three main tasks, we train two different linear probing models, one is on the probability distribution over the first token while the other is on the log-probability. As shown Fig. 7(a-f), using the probability distribution leads a significant drop in performance, while log-probability achieves results very close to the first logit under different temperature settings. Our linear probing method can be trained on log-probability distribution of the first token, and thus, only needs limited assumption of model internals.
